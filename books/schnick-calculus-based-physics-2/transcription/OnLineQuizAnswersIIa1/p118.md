## Question 1

## 0 of 20 points

What is linear charge density? (Choose the one BEST answer.)

Selected Answer: ✗ It is the charge density that lies along a line.

Correct Answer: ✓ In the case of a distribution of charge in which all the charge is on a line, it is the charge-per-length characterizing any element of the line (with units of Coulombs per meter).

Feedback: Note that the answer "In the case of a distribution of charge in which all the charge is on a line, it is the total amount of charge on that line divided by the length of the line" is okay for the case of a uniform charge distribution, but, for any other kind of charge distribution (e.g. where the charge is more "bunched up" in some regions along the line than it is in other regions) the total charge divided by the total length just gives the average linear charge density rather than the charge density itself.

## Question 2

## 20 of 20 points

A linear charge distribution of linear charge density $\lambda(x')$ extends from $x' = 0$ to $x' = L$ on the x-axis of a Cartesian coordinate system. A student is asked to find the electric field as a function of $x$, valid for points on the x-axis at points for which $x > L$.

Notes:

a) $x'$ and $x$ are both used as the position variable for points on the x-axis. $x'$ is used to specify the position of a bit of the charge that is causing an electric field to exist elsewhere, whereas $x$ is used to specify the location at which the electric field (due to all the charge) is being calculated.

b) $\lambda(x')$ is to be read “lambda of x-prime” indicating that the linear charge density is a function of position.

The student arrives at the following integral for the x-component of the electric field at $x$, in the course of solving the problem:

$$E_x(x) = k\int_0^L \frac{\lambda(x')}{(x - x')}$$

Is there anything that is obviously wrong with the integral?

Selected Answer: ✓ Yes.

Correct Answer: ✓ Yes.

Feedback: Nice work.
