## Question 4

20 of 20 points

Is it possible for the electric field to be non-zero at a point in space where the electric potential is zero?

Selected Answer: ✓ Yes

Correct Answer: ✓ Yes

Feedback: Excellent.

Consider for instance the case of an electric potential given that is only a function of x and is given by:

$$\mathrm{V} (\mathrm{x}) = 5 \text {volts} - (5 \text {volts / m}) \mathrm{x}$$

V is thus 0 at x=1m but  $E_{x} = -dV/dx$  which is 5 volts/m everywhere, including at x=1m. Hence, V being zero does not mean that E is zero.

## Question 5

20 of 20 points

The x-component of the electric field characterized by an electric potential  $V(x,y,z)$  can be expressed as -dV/dx holding y and z constant. This would suggest that the units of E would be the units of V divided by the units of x, that is, in SI units, volts per meter. Are the units of E volts per meter? If not, what is wrong with the argument above?

Selected Answer: ✓ Yes.

Correct Answer: ✓ Yes.

Feedback: Well done.

dV is an infinitesimal electrical potential difference. It may be infinitesimal, but the difference between two values of electric potential has the same units as a single value of electric potential, namely volts, whether it is infinitesimal or not. A similar case can be made for the infinitesimal difference in position dx. The quantity dx is a distance, infinitesimal though it may be. As such, it has units of distance. Note that the units of electric field are indeed N/C but this does not rule out V/m as units of electric field. In fact a volt is a J/C, and, since a joule is a Nm, a volt is also a Nm/C. Replacing the V (volts) with Nm/C in the units V/m (volts per meter) yields (Nm/C)/m which is equivalent to N/C, the SI units that we normally think of the electric field as having. In summary a V/m is a N/C so the argument that E can't have units of V/m because it has units of N/C is clearly wrong.
