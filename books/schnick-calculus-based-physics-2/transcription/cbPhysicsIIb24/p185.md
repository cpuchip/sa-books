which I copy here for your convenience:

$$\vec{\mathbf{B}} = \mu_\mathrm{o} \epsilon_\mathrm{o} v^2 \vec{\mathbf{B}}$$

Again, it is one and the same $\vec{\mathbf{B}}$ on both sides, so, the only way this equation can be true is if $\mu_\mathrm{o} \epsilon_\mathrm{o} v^2$ is exactly equal to $1$. Let's see where that leads us:

$$\mu_\mathrm{o} \epsilon_\mathrm{o} v^2 = 1$$

$$v^2 = \frac{1}{\mu_\mathrm{o} \epsilon_\mathrm{o}}$$

$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_\mathrm{o} \epsilon_\mathrm{o}}}$$

$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\left( 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \frac{\mathrm{T} \cdot \mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{A}} \right) 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \frac{\mathrm{C}^2}{\mathrm{N} \cdot \mathrm{m}^2}}}$$

$$v = 3.00 \times 10^8 \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$$

Wow! That's the speed of light! When James Clerk Maxwell found out that electric and magnetic fields propagate through space at the (already known) speed of light he realized that light is electromagnetic waves.
