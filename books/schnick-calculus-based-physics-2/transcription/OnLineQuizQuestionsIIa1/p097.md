Regarding cases involving the application of the Biot-Savart Law to calculate the magnetic field due to an electric current, which of the following statements about why it is relatively easy to calculate the magnetic field due to a circular current loop, at the center of that loop, is most correct?

The $d\vec{\ell}$ and the $\vec{r}$ in the cross product $d\vec{\ell} \times \vec{r}$ are always at right angles to each other so the magnitude of the cross product is simply $r\, d\ell$, but, one still has to deal with the fact that the vector $\vec{r}$ has a different magnitude for different elements of the current loop.

The vector $\vec{r}$ that appears in the numerator of the Biot-Savart law and the magnitude of that same vector appears in the denominator. For the case in question that vector $\vec{r}$ has the same magnitude for every current element of the circle, but, one still has to deal with the fact that because the angle between $d\vec{\ell}$ and $\vec{r}$ depends on the choice of the element of current element in the circle, the handling of the cross product $d\vec{\ell} \times \vec{r}$ is a challenge.

For the case in question, $\vec{r}$ has the same magnitude for every current element, and, $d\vec{\ell} \times \vec{r}$ reduces to $r\, d\ell$.

From among those cases in which one is calculating the magnetic field due to a current-carrying wire that lies in a plane, at a point in the same plane, the case at hand is actually one of the more difficult cases. For instance, the case in which one is to determine the magnetic field at a point in the first quadrant of a Cartesian coordinate system due to a piece of straight current-carrying wire that lies in the x-y plane is much easier than the case in question.
